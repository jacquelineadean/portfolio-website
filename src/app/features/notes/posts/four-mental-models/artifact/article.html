Four Mental Models

System design rounds at AI frontier labs and FAANG companies have no test cases, only an interviewer's mental model of a good answer. This episode lays out four mental models that work on any open-ended prompt: understand the question, pick the right building blocks, go deep where it counts, and course-correct as you talk. Judgment and depth make up 62 percent of the score, and both grade the quality of reasoning.

Overview

What the episode covers

The episode's central claim is that most candidates who fail system design rounds know the material. What they lack is a method for thinking through an open-ended problem while an interviewer watches and grades. Earlier episodes in the series each walked one specific question. This one steps up a level, to four mental models that apply to any prompt.

This page follows the four models in order. Where a model touches a technical idea, it goes further into the mechanism and the numbers: capacity math, latency costs and tradeoff pairs, plus labs that run Kafka partitioning, PagedAttention memory, batching and resharding in your browser.

Mental model 1

1

Suggested pacing · minutes 0 to 8 of a 45-minute round

Understand the question

The first model has four tools: an eight-item requirements checklist, the consumer question, a naive solution offered first, and a direct request for a hint.

A design prompt is underspecified on purpose. The interviewer holds the missing constraints, and these four tools pull them out before anything is drawn.

Turn the answers into numbers

The answers are only useful once they become arithmetic. Users and requests per day give an arrival rate. Little's law turns that rate into concurrency: the number of requests in flight equals the arrival rate times the time each one spends in the system, L = λW. For an LLM service, concurrency then turns into memory, because every live stream keeps its attention keys and values on the GPU.

That memory is easy to compute from a model's config. Each token stores one key and one value vector per layer, for each key-value head: 2 × layers × KV heads × head size × 2 bytes at 16-bit precision. Llama 3 8B needs 128 KiB per token. Llama 2 13B, which has no grouped-query attention, needs 800 KiB. That factor of six is a design decision you can say out loud.

The consumer question

The episode gives one question its own line: the consumer question. Who consumes the output, and what do they do with it? The figure below runs it on one prompt, an LLM inference service, for three different consumers. The rest of the design follows from the answer, and so do the numbers you write next to it.

Offer the naive solution first

The third tool sounds backwards. Put a deliberately simple design on the board early, then watch where the interviewer pushes. The episode frames it as a trap: the naive design gets the interviewer to signal which direction they care about. Each push shows where they want you to go.

Ask for the hint

The last tool is the most direct. The episode calls a direct hint request a Staff+ move. A staff engineer already asks stakeholders what matters before spending a week on the wrong part, so the same question in an interview reads as seniority. It costs one sentence.

Say it this way

“I can go deeper on the write path or on failure handling. Which matters more for your team?”

Mental model 2

2

Suggested pacing · minutes 8 to 18

Pick the right building blocks

The second model is about parts. The episode asks for 40 primitives memorized, and pairs the list with an order of choosing: start from the access pattern, pick the primitive that serves it, and name a vendor last.

That order is easy to check from the outside. A candidate who says “Kafka” before saying what the data looks like has skipped two steps, and the interviewer hears it.

What each primitive costs

Knowing a primitive includes knowing its price in time. A cache hit in the same process costs about a memory reference. The same hit over the network costs a datacenter round trip, about 5,000 times longer. Synchronous replication to another continent adds a round trip that is 300 times longer again. These ratios decide designs before any vendor is named.

Every choice is a pair

The rubric pays most for judgment, and judgment shows up as tradeoffs. A tradeoff is two primitives that solve the same access pattern at different prices. Saying which one you picked, what it costs, and when you would switch is the judgment line on the rubric.

Mental model 3

3

Suggested pacing · minutes 18 to 36

Go deeper, on purpose

The third model decides how far down to go, and where. The episode describes five layers of depth and says to cascade through them consciously. In practice that means knowing which layer you are on, and choosing to go down.

Knowing a component usually covers layers 1 to 3. Layers 4 and 5 hold the numbers and the failures, and that is where depth is graded. Cascading consciously means saying out loud which layer you are on and why you are going down.

Say it this way

“That's its role. What matters for this design is how it fails, so let me go there.”

Here is what going down looks like for each of the three components. Each lab runs the real mechanism in your browser: Kafka's own key hash, vLLM's 16-token blocks, and three ways to place shards.

Spend the depth budget

The episode gives a three-part test for where to spend the depth budget: components that are load-bearing, components that are novel, and components close to the interviewer's own team. Depth costs minutes, which is why it needs a budget. Drawn as circles, the test puts the deepest dive where all three overlap.

Breadth works against you

The chapter's counter-intuitive rule: breadth is negatively correlated with level. Covering more components tends to signal a lower level. One mechanism is the clock. Minutes spent placing a twelfth box are minutes not spent on the reasoning that makes up 62 percent of the score.

Mental model 4

4

Suggested pacing · the whole round, and minutes 36 to 45

Discuss, and course-correct

The fourth model covers the conversation itself. The episode lists six interviewer signals to read, a three-second detector for a wrong direction, five escape hatches whose exact phrasing the host says to memorize, and a way to end strong.

A design round is a dialogue, and the interviewer gives away information all the way through it.

The three-second check

The episode describes a three-second wrong-direction detector. After each reaction from the interviewer, take three seconds to decide whether you are still on their path. The game below gives you six reactions and three seconds for each.

Five escape hatches

The episode lists five escape hatches and says to memorize the exact phrasings. Here are five drafts. Copy them, then rewrite them in your own voice before an interview.

Fail one box at a time

A strong ending names the biggest risk and how you would see it coming. The fastest way to find it is to fail each box in turn and ask three questions. What do users see? Which metric moves first? What limits the damage?

End strong

The episode closes the fourth model with how to end strong. One form that works is a four-line summary in the final two minutes, aimed at judgment and depth, the two rubric lines worth 62 points.

Debrief

The bar-raiser observation

Almost never a knowledge gap

The episode ends where it began. The host's bar-raiser observation: every rejection you have seen in a system design round is almost certainly a mental-model failure, and almost never a knowledge failure. The notes end the line with two words: every one.

Read against the four models, a candidate who names components without their costs fails twice: in the second model, which picks blocks for stated reasons, and in the fourth, which talks those reasons through.
